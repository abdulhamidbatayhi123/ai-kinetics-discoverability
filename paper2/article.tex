\begin{abstract}
Equation-discovery methods aim to learn governing laws from data, but how much more they need
than fitting an assumed law is rarely measured. We measured it in a pre-registered computational
study of adsorption columns. Across four decades of the Damk\"ohler number Da (uptake rate relative to front passage),
dispersion, sensor noise and isotherm error, we compared where the best of several sparse and symbolic regression
methods stopped recovering the uptake rate law with where its rate constant stopped being
identifiable. Wherever both limits fell within the tested range (37 conditions), discovery failed
first, by 1.6 to 3.7 decades at 200 observations per run and by 0.7 to 2.0 decades in three
conditions re-run at 2,000. A 5\,\% isotherm error prevented discovery at every rate, while the rate constant stayed
identifiable. Column data can calibrate an assumed rate law long after
they can no longer reveal it.
\end{abstract}

% ---- Introduction (no heading in the Article format; target about 550 words) ----
Equation-discovery methods aim to learn a governing law itself from data, not only its
coefficients: sparse regression over a library of candidate terms \citep{brunton2016,rudy2016}
and its weak-form \citep{messenger2021ode,reinbold2020}, ensemble \citep{fasel2022} and exact
best-subset \citep{bertsimas2022} variants, symbolic regression
\citep{cranmer2023,udrescu2020,ruan2026,yu2026}, bi-level identification \citep{li2025bilevel} and
neural networks embedded in the governing equations \citep{santana2023}. Discovering a law is a
stricter task than estimating its coefficient, yet the two are rarely compared on the same data.
Adsorption columns are a sharp test. A fixed-bed experiment reports concentrations and temperatures
over time, most often at the outlet, and what an engineer needs from it is the uptake rate law,
usually the linear driving force (LDF), $\partial q/\partial t = k\,(\qs(c,T) - q)$, with $q$ the
adsorbed loading, $\qs$ its equilibrium value from the isotherm, $c$ the concentration and $T$ the
temperature. For adsorption, one
study has tried this from column data, training a neural uptake term on noisy outlet curves with
the isotherm given exactly, at one rate and one noise level; for a Langmuir isotherm its readout
held the two LDF terms and a constant the true law lacks, and for a Sips isotherm symbolic
regression returned the exact LDF form \citep{santana2023}. Where such a result
holds, and where it stops, is not known. The natural scale is the Damk\"ohler number,
$\Da = k\,t_{\mathrm{stoich}}$, the number of kinetic time constants in one passage of the
adsorption front: at low $\Da$ uptake lags far behind equilibrium, at high $\Da$ it nearly keeps up.

Part of the answer is classical. Near local equilibrium the mass-transfer coefficient is
poorly determined by column data \citep{valocchi1985}: finite rates then act only through an
apparent dispersion \citep{katsoulas2023,dejaco2024}, the LDF coefficient can be extracted wrongly
from outlet breakthrough \citep{knox2016}, and most of the output variability belongs to the
isotherm \citep{ward2022,taylor2026}. Yet lumped rate coefficients are routinely estimated from
breakthrough curves \citep{miyabe2000,dejaco2025}. Batch studies warn that data near equilibrium cannot tell kinetic laws apart
\citep{canzano2012,simonin2016}.

Equation discovery has limits of its own. On a single transient that settles onto a slow
manifold, as a breakthrough run does, the candidate terms become nearly collinear
\citep{stanhope2014,gallo2026,delgado2025}. Noise induces phase transitions in what can be
recovered \citep{klishin2024,fajardo2023}, some classes of dynamics cannot be discovered at all
\citep{shumaylov2025}, and success maps against sampling and noise exist for biological data \citep{nardini2020}. Discovery has been coupled to identifiability by keeping
only identifiable models \citep{massonis2023}, identifiability has been extended from parameters
to unknown functions \citep{loman2026}, and kinetic rate laws have been discovered in reaction
engineering \citep{servia2024}. How much harder discovery is, where, for which methods, and from which measurements
has not, to our knowledge, been measured.

The question matters most where the law is not the textbook one. In the water-harvesting
framework MOF-303 the water diffusion rate passes through a minimum at the steep step of the
isotherm, where the LDF approximation is least accurate \citep{lassitter2024,hastings2024}, and a
loading-dependent rate has been fitted by hand \citep{abuelmaaty2024}.

We asked, in a pre-registered computational study, for which regimes the adsorption rate law
can be discovered from data a column experiment can produce, and whether discovery fails at the
same $\Da$ as the practical identifiability of the rate constant \citep{raue2009}, or
earlier. The rate constant is practically identifiable when the data, fitted with the true law,
confine it to within a factor of two. Scoring discovery requires knowing the true law, so the data
were simulated: ground truth came from a verified non-isothermal column model
\citep{paper1} for a Langmuir sorbent and a MOF-303-like step isotherm. The design spanned four
decades of $\Da$, three levels of axial dispersion (P\'eclet numbers), four noise levels and four
levels of isotherm error. The column was observed in three ways (Fig.~1): interior probes of
concentration, uptake and temperature (O1), the same probes without uptake (O2), and the outlet
alone (O3). Methods from each family above were run in the
strongest configuration we could verify within declared cost limits, and identifiability was
judged by profile likelihood on the same observations. Discovery succeeded when a method returned
exactly the terms of the true law with the right signs and coefficient ratio. In each slice of the
design (one isotherm, P\'eclet number, noise level and isotherm error) we located two boundaries,
the $\Da$ at which discovery and identifiability fall to 50\,\% success, and called their distance
in decades the gap.
The design, thresholds and verdict words were frozen before any method ran on the grid;
analyses chosen after the verdicts are labelled post hoc. In all 37 of the 96 slices in which both boundaries fell within the tested range, discovery failed
first, 1.6 to 3.7 decades of $\Da$ before the rate constant stopped being identifiable, and in the
three slices re-run with ten times more observations it still failed first.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Discovery fails long before identifiability}
We report the pre-registered comparison first, then what moves the boundaries, which methods
discover the law, denser sampling, observation without probes of uptake, and a loading-dependent
rate.
Both isotherms passed the positive control (lowest $\Da$, no noise or isotherm error): the law
was recovered, and $k$ identifiable, in 135 of 135 replicates for each. As $\Da$ grows, the
driving force $\qs - q$ that distinguishes the law shrinks below the noise, while the curves still
constrain $k$ (Fig.~2).
% results/p2_verdicts.json positive_control (langmuir, step: success_fraction 1.0,
% identifiable_fraction 1.0, n 135, need 0.9); results/p2_verdicts_asrun.json (step failed).

The confirmatory test (H2b) compares, in each slice, the $\Da$ at which the best discovery method
falls to 50\,\% success, $\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{disc}}$, with the $\Da$ at which $k$ stops being
identifiable in half the replicates, $\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{ident}}$. Of the 96 slices, 37 had both
boundaries on the grid. In all 37 the verdict was \verdict{discovery fails before
identifiability}, so H2b's pre-registered overall word is the same (Fig.~3, Table~1; every slice
in Extended Data Figs.~1 and~2). The gap was 1.6 to 3.7 decades of $\Da$, with a median of 2.2
decades for the Langmuir sorbent (23 slices) and 2.5 for the step isotherm (14 slices); allowing
for boundaries known only to lie between two levels, every gap is at least 1.35 decades (Methods).
Discovery failed between $\Da = 0.11$ and 13, while $k$ stayed identifiable up to $\Da$ between 43
and 994. 
The best
discovery method was weak-form exact best-subset selection in 34 of the 37 slices and the
weak-form ensemble in the other three.

Noise moved both boundaries, but not equally. With 0.5\,\% noise and an exact isotherm, discovery
held to $\Da \approx 4$ to 7 and identifiability to $\Da \approx 320$ to 740; at 2\,\% noise
discovery fell to $\Da \approx 1$, and at 5\,\% to $\Da \approx 0.1$ to 0.3, while identifiability
stayed between $\Da \approx 57$ and 172. A small isotherm error hurt discovery more than noise
did. At 0.5\,\% noise, adding a 0.5\,\% isotherm error moved $\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{disc}}$ from about 5
to about 0.1 to 0.2 (in the three slices that kept a boundary), while $\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{ident}}$
moved by less than 0.2 decade. With isotherm error, success was not monotone in noise: at 2\,\%
isotherm error the best method reached at most 18 of 45 replicates without noise but up to 43 with
noise: without noise, weak-form best-subset selection kept on average 3.6 to 4.0 terms (four is
the maximum) at $\Da \le 10$, absorbing the isotherm error, until noise lifted the residual above
the selection criterion's floor (post hoc).

\subsection*{The two other confirmatory tests could not be decided}
The second confirmatory test (H2a) asked whether one ratio, the realised driving force over the
combined observation and isotherm error ($R = \delta/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$), places the
discovery boundary at the same $R^{*}$ in every condition. Only one of twelve conditions crossed
50\,\% in $R$ ($R^{*} = 123$, 95\,\% interval 113 to 135), so the test has no verdict
(Extended Data Fig.~4).

\subsection*{Which methods discover the law, and where}
Exact best-subset selection in the weak form was the most reliable method: its success
crossed 50\,\% within the grid in 36 of the 96 slices, against 8 for weak-form sequential
thresholding and 7 for the weak-form ensemble (Fig.~4c). No strong-form sparse
method had a crossing in any slice. Symbolic
regression, run only at $\Pe \approx 94$ with noise of 0.5 or 2\,\% and isotherm error
of 0 or 2\,\%, behaved differently from all the sparse methods. It failed at the lowest rates,
where weak-form sparse regression worked best, and succeeded at intermediate rates: up to
$\Da \approx 32$ without isotherm error, and between $\Da = 1$ and 10 with 2\,\% isotherm error,
where no sparse method reached 50\,\% (Fig.~4a,b; Extended Data Fig.~5). The pre-registered
boundary, a falling logistic curve, cannot describe such a window. If the boundary is instead taken
as the last level with at least 50\,\% success (post hoc), H2b is unchanged: in the four such
slices with a verdict, that level lay 1.0 to 1.4 decades below the identifiability boundary.
Neither family covers the range in which $k$ remains identifiable.

The neural-network pipeline of the closest prior work, run as a declared demonstration with one
replicate per level, recovered the law in one of its nine fits ($\Da = 3.2$). Implicit sparse
regression without any isotherm (SINDy-PI), the pre-registered route to the law's form on an
isothermal control, recovered it in none of 2{,}160 fits, even without noise: every fit kept six
terms, one more than the implicit Langmuir--LDF relation. Slow-manifold discovery, which targets
the isotherm, reached 50\,\% only with exact data, at $\Da = 1000$ (Extended Data Fig.~5).

Where open discovery fails, a law can still be chosen among candidates. In a post hoc
check, the LDF law, the quadratic driving force and the pseudo-second-order law were each
fitted by the likelihood used for identifiability and the best fit chosen ($\Pe \approx 94$,
noise 0.5 and 2\,\%, isotherm error 0 and 2\,\%). The LDF law was chosen in all 45 replicates at every
$\Da \le 10$ with an exact isotherm, and in at least 35 of 45 with 2\,\% isotherm error, where
sparse regression failed at every rate (Fig.~4d). It did not reach the identifiability boundary:
with an exact isotherm, the LDF law was chosen in only 31 of 180 replicates at $\Da = 31.6$ and
in none from $\Da = 100$ on; on noise-free data with up to 20 times more samples, the LDF law
won in three of the four cells tested at $\Da \ge 100$ (post hoc; Supplementary Note~1).

\subsection*{Ten times more observations narrow the gap but do not close it}
The boundaries above assume 200 observation times per run. With 400 (post hoc), every boundary
moved to higher $\Da$ or stayed, and discovery still failed at least 1.2 decades first. With about
2,000, on the $\Pe \approx 94$ cells of both isotherms at 0.5 and 2\,\% noise (post hoc, declared
before it ran; Supplementary Note~1), discovery moved up by 0.7 to 1.9 decades and identifiability by 0.2 to
0.5 decade. Discovery still failed first in the three slices with both boundaries, by 0.7 to 2.0
decades, above the 0.25-decade threshold; in the fourth (step isotherm, 0.5\,\% noise) discovery
reached $\Da \approx 310$ while $k$ stayed identifiable beyond the grid. At fixed noise per
sample, the extra observations add information as well as resolution, and this check does not
separate the two.

\subsection*{Without probes of uptake, $k$ stays identifiable but the law is not discovered}
With concentration and temperature probes but no uptake (O2), uptake must first be
reconstructed from the gas-phase mass balance. Discovery then failed everywhere: no method
succeeded in more than 29\,\% of the replicates at any level of any slice.

\subsection*{A loading-dependent rate passes the test almost only at $\Da = 1$}
For the step isotherm the study also simulated a rate that falls to 20\,\% of its value across
the isotherm step (L2), a form motivated by the measurements in MOF-303; this part is
exploratory. By the pre-registered rule, the state dependence was discovered almost only at
$\Da = 1$. There the best method succeeded in 77 to 100\,\% of all 60 replicates at every noise
level and isotherm error; at any other level it reached at most 58\,\%, and without noise it never
succeeded at $\Da \le 0.32$ (Extended Data Fig.~6). These are best values over methods and
P\'eclet numbers, so they bound any single method from above. A likely reason lies in the test
itself: the library cannot
represent a rate falling sigmoidally across the step, so success follows which polynomial terms
are selected (Methods).

\section*{Discussion}
Column data can calibrate an assumed rate law long after they can no longer reveal it: the law
could be discovered only over a narrow part of the range in which its rate constant could be
measured, 1.6 to 3.7 decades of $\Da$ short of it at 200 observations per run. For practice this sets an order of operations in three steps (Fig.~5). First, where
uptake is slow compared with the passage of the front ($\Da$ of order one or below) and the
isotherm is known almost exactly, weak-form sparse regression can propose the law; with as little
as 0.5\,\% noise and 0.5\,\% isotherm error, every discovery boundary fell to $\Da \le 0.23$.
Second, at that sampling and up to $\Da \approx 10$, the law can still be chosen among a few candidates by likelihood,
even with 2\,\% isotherm error. Third, beyond $\Da \approx 10$ the law has to be assumed. The data
still give its coefficient: from interior probes for one and a half to nearly four decades of
$\Da$ beyond the discovery boundary, and, in the few conditions tested, at least as far from the
outlet alone. Denser sampling moves all three thresholds up, and a misplaced isotherm step is the
exception (see the last paragraph).

Discovery is more fragile because the two tasks ask different things of the same data.
Identifiability asks whether a known law with one free coefficient fits well only near the true
value. Discovery asks the data to choose that law among many and to return the coefficients of
$\qsm$ and $q$ in the ratio $-1$ within 10\,\%. An isotherm
error enters discovery as an error in a regressor, which biases exactly that ratio. In the
identifiability fit the same smooth error is absorbed into $k$, and the estimate stays within a
factor of two of the truth. The response to a 0.5\,\% isotherm error (over a decade for discovery, under 0.2
for identifiability) fits this reading, though the study was not designed to test it.
The asymmetry of the two success rules does not make the gap: dropping the ratio test, requiring
the interval to lie within 10\,\% of $k$, or judging by the point estimate alone kept every verdict,
with gaps of 0.89 to 3.75 decades (post hoc).
% results/p2_e4_criteria.json (EXPLORATORY_P2 E4; p2_e4_criteria.py): variants a (discovery relaxed)
% 40 slices, gaps 1.60-3.72; b (interval in [0.9k, 1.1k]) 37, 0.89-2.94; c (point estimate in
% [k/2, 2k]) 37, 1.79-3.75; d (a and c) 40, 1.79-3.75; every slice "fails before"; controls pass.
A second effect is classical: as $\Da$ grows, uptake approaches equilibrium and the law's two
terms become nearly collinear on the slow manifold \citep{delgado2025,gallo2026}. In a post hoc
check, the smaller singular value of the standardised pair $\{q, \qsm\}$ fell from about 0.75 of
the larger at $\Da = 0.1$ to about 0.001 at $\Da = 1000$. Exact data still separate the two terms,
which is why discovery never failed with exact data (no noise and no isotherm error), but noise and
isotherm error soon exceed the shrinking direction that distinguishes them. A one-parameter fit
needs no such direction: it uses the whole curve to place $k$. We expect the same asymmetry
wherever a law is to be discovered alongside an imperfectly known constitutive input, such as an
equation of state or a transport property, but this study tested only adsorption.

% p2_e5_conditioning.py (EXPLORATORY_P2 E5).

Real columns sit mostly beyond the discovery boundaries. Published data for water on MOF-303
\citep{lassitter2024} and carbon dioxide on zeolite 13X \citep{dantas2011} or MIL-91(Ti)
\citep{krishnamurthy2025} place physisorbent packed beds between $\Da \approx 100$ and 23{,}000
(order-of-magnitude estimates; Supplementary Table~1). An amine bed for
direct air capture lay at $\Da \approx 8$ to 10 \citep{stampi2024}, within reach of the highest
discovery boundaries at 200 observations per run ($\Da \approx 7$ with noise, 13 without). With 200 observations per run the
law must be chosen or assumed for physisorbent beds; with 2,000 and 0.5\,\% noise, discovery
reached the lower end of their range.

equations (pre-registered, computed after the verdicts), its columns ran at $\Da \approx 8.8$

boundary we found with noise at 200 observations per run (it used 55). Under our rule its Langmuir readout (the LDF terms plus a constant)
fails, but its Sips readout, $0.198\,\qs - 0.200\,q$, succeeds. That is one realisation of a different model; a replicated test there would decide.

Whether a loading-dependent law such as that of water in MOF-303
\citep{lassitter2024,hastings2024} can be discovered remains open. Our library could not represent
a sigmoidal $k(q)$, so that test measured which polynomial terms were selected rather than whether
such a law is discoverable.

All data were simulated by one verified column model with the LDF law as the truth, so the
boundaries hold for that model, the two isotherms and the error models used; no second physical
system was tested. Real columns add errors in geometry, dispersion and heat transfer that were
held exact here, and real isotherms are rarely known to 0.5 to 5\,\%; both would be expected to
lower the discovery boundaries, as would a larger candidate library. Interior probes of uptake
(O1) are a ceiling that experiments rarely reach. The identifiability criterion measures precision
and accuracy, not calibration: the intervals contained the true $k$ in only about 10\,\% of O1 rows
(23\,\% on O3; exploratory; Extended Data Fig.~7), yet judged by the point estimate alone every
verdict holds. Discovery gains most from denser sampling, so the boundaries at 200 observations per
run understate what a densely sampled experiment can discover.
Symbolic regression ran on a subgrid, the neural pipeline only as a demonstration, and a
Kolmogorov--Arnold network only in an exploratory run, where it recovered the law in none of 216
fits; each limit was set by cost rules declared before the grid ran, on the one computer
available, and the larger neural pipeline declared for a graphics processor was not run.
Combining method families was not tested, nor were errors-in-variables fitting in the weak form or
recent methods such as parallel symbolic enumeration \citep{ruan2026} and bi-level identification
\citep{li2025bilevel}.

MOF-303-like isotherm by 1\,\% relative humidity kept discovery at or below 31\,\% at every rate,

An isotherm whose step is placed to better than 1\,\% relative humidity is worth more than quieter
sensors. And the map should be tested on experimental breakthrough data with interior
probes at moderate $\Da$, where it predicts that discovery first becomes possible.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Ground truth}
Ground truth came from the finite-difference reference solver of the companion
study \citep{paper1}: a one-dimensional, axially dispersed, non-isothermal model of a
packed column, verified there against closed-form solutions. Uptake followed one of two
laws,
\begin{align}
\text{L1:}\quad & \frac{\partial q}{\partial t} = k\,\bigl(\qs(c,T) - q\bigr),\\
\text{L2:}\quad & \frac{\partial q}{\partial t} = k(q)\,\bigl(\qs(c,T) - q\bigr),\qquad
k(q) = k_0\Bigl[1 - 0.8\,s\!\Bigl(\frac{q/q_{\max}-\theta}{0.05}\Bigr)\Bigr],
\end{align}
where $s$ is the logistic function and $\theta$ is the fractional loading at the
isotherm's step midpoint, so that under L2 the rate falls to 20\,\% of $k_0$ across the step.
% PREREG_P2 s.4.1; p2_generate.py (L2_DROP 0.8, L2_WIDTH 0.05, step_midpoint_fraction).
Two adsorbents were simulated with the same column geometry and flow (Supplementary
Table~2): the companion study's favourable Langmuir sorbent, and water on a MOF-303-like
framework, a cooperative (Type V) isotherm with its step at $P/P_0 = 0.15$, fed at 30\,\%
relative humidity and 30\,$^\circ$C. The Damk\"ohler number $\Da = k\,t_{\mathrm{stoich}}$
counts kinetic time constants per passage of the adsorption front, with
$t_{\mathrm{stoich}}$ the stoichiometric time of the column (7200\,s for Langmuir and
31\,050\,s for the step isotherm). Nine levels from $10^{-1}$ to $10^{3}$ at half-decade
spacing therefore span $k$ from $1.39\times10^{-5}$ to $0.139$\,s$^{-1}$ for Langmuir and
from $3.22\times10^{-6}$ to $0.0322$\,s$^{-1}$ for the step isotherm. Three axial-dispersion
coefficients, $D_L \times 0.2$, $\times 1$ and $\times 5$ of the default, completed the grid.
They give column P\'eclet numbers $\Pe = uL/D_L$ of about 470, 94 and 19, and the levels are
named by these values below (the code and the pre-registration label them by the $D_L$
multiplier, so its ``$\times 0.2$'' level is the highest $\Pe$). The grid has 81 solves at 400 cells and 400 stored times, each run until the outlet reached 95\,\%
breakthrough, plus 9 isothermal solves for a control described below. The manifest that
records every parameter is public.

\subsection*{Observation models, noise and isotherm error}
Every method and every identifiability calculation read the data through one function,
so all saw the same corrupted observations. Three observation models were used: interior
probes of $c$, $q$ and $T$ (O1), interior probes of $c$ and $T$ with $q$ hidden (O2), and the
outlet $c(t)$ and $T(t)$ only (O3). O1 and O2 used 20 equally spaced probes; all models
used 200 times spread evenly over the solved horizon. Noise was independent Gaussian with
standard deviation $\sigma \in \{0, 0.5, 2, 5\}\,\%$ of each channel's range. Methods were
never given the true isotherm. They were given a measured one,
$\qsm(c,T) = \qs(c,T)\,[1 + \varepsilon\, g(c/c_{\mathrm{in}})]$ with
$\varepsilon \in \{0, 0.5, 2, 5\}\,\%$, where $g$ is a smooth random function of four
Fourier modes on $[0, 1]$, scaled to unit root-mean-square and drawn once per replicate.
Every random draw was seeded by a hash: the noise by (cell, replicate, $\sigma$, observation
model) and the isotherm error by (cell, replicate), so each observation can be regenerated on
its own and every observation model sees the same measured isotherm. A replicate, one such
observation of one cell, was the unit of analysis. Discovery on O1 and O2 and identifiability
on O1 used 60 replicates per cell; the costlier O2 and O3 identifiability profiles used 3, and
the M1 demonstration 1.

\subsection*{Discovery methods}
Each method ran in the strongest configuration we could verify on known-answer systems
before the pre-registration was frozen, and a method that could not be run that way was
reported as such rather than replaced by a weaker one. The candidate library was
$\{1, c, q, T, cq, q^2, c^2, \qsm\}$; under L1 the true law is $k(\qsm - q)$. The methods
were sparse regression on a Savitzky--Golay estimate of $\mathrm{d}q/\mathrm{d}t$ (strong
form) and on its weak, integral form with compactly supported test functions
\citep{messenger2021ode,messenger2021pde}, each with three solvers: sequentially thresholded
least squares \citep{brunton2016}, a bagged ensemble with an inclusion threshold of 0.6
\citep{fasel2022}, and exact best-subset selection by enumeration (M4; exact subset selection
as in \citep{bertsimas2022}, here scored by the Bayesian information criterion); errors-in-variables
best-subset regression given the true error levels (M5, an oracle); implicit sparse
regression without any isotherm on an isothermal control (M6, SINDy-PI \citep{kaheman2020});
symbolic regression (M7, PySR \citep{cranmer2023}); a universal differential equation with a
neural network in the uptake term, trained through the column model and read out by sparse
and symbolic regression (M1, the pipeline of \citep{santana2023}); and regression of the state
on $q$-free features, which returns the isotherm where uptake is at equilibrium (M9
\citep{delgado2025}). On O2, uptake was first recovered from the gas-phase mass balance,
and the three sparse solvers were then run on it in both forms; M5, M7 and M9 were not run on
O2, as the pre-registered applicability table states.
% p2_grid_o23.py (SOLVERS x FORMS); PREREG_P2 s.3 applicability table. M1 could be trained for at most 50 L-BFGS steps or
2\,h per fit on the machine available, so it ran as a declared demonstration: Langmuir,
$\Pe \approx 94$, $\sigma = 2\,\%$, $\varepsilon = 0$, O3, the nine $\Da$ levels, one
replicate. The best parameters seen during training were kept. As first run, a column solve
that exhausted its step budget during training ended the fit with no readout (four of the
nine fits); the corrected code keeps the best parameters in that case, and both runs are
reported (Supplementary Note~2). A Kolmogorov--Arnold network \citep{liu2024} failed its known-answer test and its
cost gate before the freeze and was not run on the grid. Method settings, solver thresholds
and their known-answer tests are listed in Supplementary Note~3.

% p2_grid_extra.py (fit_m1: M1_MAXITER 50, M1_BUDGET_S 7200; _train_lbfgs, B88);

\subsection*{Success}
Discovery succeeded when the selected terms were exactly those of the true law, every sign
was correct and, for L1, the ratio of the $\qsm$ and $q$ coefficients was $-1$ within
10\,\%. The value of $k$ itself was not required. For L2 (H2d), the library holds no term for a
rate falling sigmoidally across the step, so the rule asks instead whether $\qsm$ and $q$ are
selected and the rate implied by the selected terms, $-\partial f/\partial q$ averaged in ten bins
of loading, decreases across the bins (Spearman $\rho < -0.8$) to at most 60\,\% of its value in
the lowest bin. A method that raised an error counted as a failure; no replicate, cell or output
was excluded.
% p2/metric.py success_L1, success_L2 (n_bins 10, rho_max -0.8, drop_max 0.6); PREREG_P2 s.4.3 and A2.

\subsection*{Identifiability}
Practical identifiability of $k$ was judged by profile likelihood \citep{raue2009} with the
true law known, on the same observations and with the same measured isotherm as the
discovery methods: $k$ was practically identifiable when the 95\,\% profile interval
(likelihood-ratio threshold 3.84) lay within $[k/2, 2k]$. On O1 the likelihood summed over
all probes the fit of the known law, integrated exactly for piecewise-linear $\qs(t)$ along
the observed $c$ and $T$. On O2 and O3 every point of the profile was a full column solve.
The O1 likelihood involves only the local uptake law along the observed $c$ and $T$, so errors in
dispersion, heat transfer or geometry do not enter it; on O2 and O3 the column model is taken as
exact, as it is in the simulations.
The profile searched a coarse logarithmic grid of $k$ and refined it around the interval
(41 points per pass on O1, 21 on O2 and O3, as pre-registered).
Three corrections to the identifiability profiles were made during the study and are reported
in full (Supplementary Note~2). First, on O2 and O3 a measured isotherm with $\varepsilon > 0$ could stall the stiff
solver at large trial $k$ for days; each solve was therefore capped at 600\,s and a capped
solve was scored as a failed solve (amendment A5; two O3 rows had capped solves, none next to
its interval). Second, the O1 grid first spanned $10^{-5}$ to $10$\,s$^{-1}$, which excluded the
step cells at $\Da = 0.1$; it was widened to $10^{-7}$ to $10$\,s$^{-1}$, a peak on the grid
edge was made an error, and all O1 rows were recomputed (amendment A6). Before that
recomputation, the O1 refinement was found to use 21 points per pass instead of the
pre-registered 41 and was set to 41, so every corrected O1 row uses the declared settings.
Third, a later review of the code found that the O1 update, meant to be exact, carried a wrong
coefficient: it differed from the exact solution only by a term of order $(kh)^2$ ($h$ the
interval between observations), so a check made at small $kh$ had passed, but it misfit the data when $kh$ was of
order one or larger, that is, at high $\Da$. The update was corrected and tested against a
numerical ODE solver for $kh$ from $10^{-4}$ to 100, and all 51\,840 O1 rows were recomputed with
the analysis unchanged (amendment A7 records this correction and the refinement setting).

\subsection*{Boundaries and verdicts}
For each slice the boundary $\Da^{*}$ was the 50\,\% crossing of an unpenalised logistic
regression of replicate outcomes on $\log_{10}\Da$, with a percentile bootstrap interval
(2000 resamples within each $\Da$ level). No boundary was reported, and no verdict issued,
when the fitted curve did not cross 50\,\% inside the tested range or rose with $\Da$ instead
of falling; the range was reported instead. The boundary is the crossing of the fitted curve, so
a slice whose observed success stays just below 50\,\% can still have one: in one slice
(Langmuir, $\Pe \approx 19$, 2\,\% noise and isotherm error) success was 0.49 at the two lowest
levels and the curve crosses 50\,\% at $\Da = 0.16$. In all, 10 of the 37 discovery boundaries lie
within 0.25 decade of the lowest level, $\Da = 0.1$; their slices have gaps of 2.3 to 3.7
decades, so the narrowest gaps do not depend on them. Before the freeze the estimator's coverage on planted boundaries was 0.94 for a
nominal 0.95, with a bias of $+0.012$ decade. The best discovery method of each slice was
chosen on replicates 0--14 and its boundary estimated on replicates 15--59, which removes the
inflation that choosing and estimating on the same replicates causes (0.07 decade in
simulation); the identifiability boundary was estimated on the same 45 replicates. The best
method was the one with the largest crossing on the selection replicates, or, if none crossed,
the one with the highest mean success. Only rate-law methods that ran on every cell of the
slice competed: M5, which knows the true error levels, and M9, which targets the isotherm, were
excluded.

Three hypotheses were confirmatory, each with verdict words fixed in advance. H2a: success
collapses onto one curve in $R = \delta/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$, the driving force
relative to the combined error, with the spread of $R^{*}$ across conditions under half a
decade. H2b: on O1, for each slice ($\sigma$, $\varepsilon$, isotherm, $\Pe$; 96 in all),
the gap $\log_{10}\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{disc}} - \log_{10}\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{ident}}$ was called
\verdict{discovery fails before identifiability} at $-0.25$ decade or less,
\verdict{discovery outlives identifiability} at $+0.25$ or more, and
\verdict{discovery fails with identifiability} in between; the hypothesis-level verdict was
the word held by at least two thirds of the slices with a verdict. Slices share ground-truth
solves and isotherm-error draws, so they are not independent, and the two-thirds rule summarises
them without a formal error rate. H2c: errors-in-variables
regression moves the discovery boundary upwards by at least 0.25 decade in at least two thirds
of the slices with a verdict at a given $\varepsilon > 0$, tested at $\sigma = 2\,\%$ on O1 in the
strong form against the best strong-form method that is not errors-in-variables, in 18 slices
(three $\varepsilon$, two isotherms, three P\'eclet numbers); a slice has a verdict only if both
estimators have a boundary on the grid, and the refined grid placed around the comparator's
boundary is solved only if that boundary exists. With 45 estimation replicates the H2b rule
returned a wrong directional verdict with probability 0.001 when there was no gap, and the
correct one with probability 0.965 at a gap of 0.40 decade, the smallest effect of interest.
A positive control guarded every verdict: in each isotherm's easiest cells (lowest $\Da$,
$\sigma = \varepsilon = 0$, O1, the three P\'eclet numbers pooled, where the pre-registration
names one easiest cell) the best method had to
succeed and $k$ had to be identifiable in at least 90\,\% of the estimation replicates, or that
isotherm's verdicts were withheld. O2 and O3 were
reported descriptively, and H2d, whether the loading-dependent rate of L2 can be discovered,
was exploratory.

\subsection*{Boundaries with perfect separation}
Where every replicate is identifiable at one level of $\Da$ and none at the next, the
unpenalised logistic fit has no finite maximum-likelihood slope: the optimiser stops at a
steep curve whose crossing lies between the two levels, near their geometric midpoint. Every
bootstrap resample reproduces the same data, so the interval has zero width. Such a boundary
is reported as lying between those two levels; the gap used by H2b is computed from it exactly
as from any other crossing. Such a boundary is interval-censored. This was so for the
identifiability boundary in 18 of the 37 slices with a verdict; placing each of these at the
lower of its two levels, nearest the discovery boundary, every gap is still at least 1.35
decades, so no verdict depends on where in its interval the boundary lies, and the gaps quoted
in the main text are point estimates within these bounds.

\subsection*{Statistics and reproducibility}
The unit of analysis was a replicate, one independently seeded observation of one simulated cell.
Each cell had 60 replicates for O1 and O2 discovery and O1 identifiability: replicates 0--14
selected each slice's best method and replicates 15--59 estimated the boundaries. Boundaries are
50\,\% crossings of unpenalised logistic fits with 95\,\% percentile bootstrap intervals (2,000
resamples within levels). No replicate, cell or output was excluded; errors counted as failures.
All random draws are seeded by hashes of their keys, so any observation can be regenerated alone,
and the design, thresholds and verdict words were fixed before the grid ran.
