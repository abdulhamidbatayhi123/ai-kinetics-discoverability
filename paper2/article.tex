\begin{abstract}
Equation-discovery methods promise to learn the uptake rate law of an adsorption column from
its data, but where they can is unknown. In a pre-registered computational study spanning
four decades of Damk\"ohler number (Da), three P\'eclet numbers, noise and isotherm error, we
compared the Da at which the best discovery method stopped recovering the linear-driving-force
law with the Da at which its rate constant stopped being practically identifiable. In all 30
conditions where both boundaries existed, discovery failed first, by 1.6 to 3.4 decades. An
isotherm error of 5\,\% prevented discovery at every rate, while the rate constant remained
identifiable from interior probes. Weak-form sparse regression worked at low Da and
symbolic regression in a window near Da 1 to 10; neither reached the range where the
rate constant stayed identifiable. Column data carry information about the rate long after its
law can no longer be discovered.
\end{abstract}

% ---- Introduction (no heading in the Article format; target about 550 words) ----
A fixed-bed adsorption experiment reports concentrations and temperatures over time, most
often at the column outlet. What an engineer needs from it is the uptake rate law, usually
written as the linear driving force (LDF), $\partial q/\partial t = k\,(\qs(c,T) - q)$.
Equation-discovery methods offer to find the law itself, not only its coefficient: sparse
regression over a library of candidate terms \citep{brunton2016,rudy2016} and its weak-form
\citep{messenger2021ode,reinbold2020}, ensemble \citep{fasel2022} and exact best-subset
\citep{bertsimas2022} variants, symbolic regression \citep{cranmer2023,udrescu2020}, and
neural networks embedded in the governing equations \citep{santana2023}. For adsorption, one
study has done this from column data: Santana et al.\ trained a neural uptake term on noisy
outlet breakthrough curves and read the LDF form back out, with the isotherm given exactly and
at one rate and one noise level \citep{santana2023}. Repeated database searches found no other
study that discovers an adsorption rate law from column data. Where such a result holds, and
where it stops holding, is not known.

Part of the answer is classical. Near local equilibrium the mass-transfer coefficient is
poorly determined by column data \citep{valocchi1985}: finite rates then act only through an
apparent dispersion \citep{katsoulas2023}, the LDF coefficient can be extracted wrongly from outlet
breakthrough \citep{knox2016}, and most of the output variability belongs to the isotherm
\citep{ward2022,taylor2026}. Yet in the constant-pattern regime a lumped rate coefficient is
recovered from breakthrough consistently with theory \citep{miyabe2000}, so any limit must be
conditioned on dispersion, isotherm shape, noise and isotherm error. Equation discovery has its
own limits. On a single transient trajectory that settles onto a slow manifold, as a
breakthrough run does, the candidate terms become nearly collinear and the data barely
distinguish them \citep{stanhope2014,gallo2026,delgado2025}; noise induces phase transitions
in what can be recovered \citep{klishin2024,fajardo2023}; and unique recovery
needs conditions that non-chaotic dynamics generally lack \citep{shumaylov2025}. These results
say that discovery must fail somewhere for column data. None says where, for which methods, or
whether it fails where the rate constant itself stops being identifiable.

The question matters most where the law is not the textbook one. In the water-harvesting
framework MOF-303 the water diffusion rate passes through a minimum at the steep
step of the isotherm, and the LDF approximation is least accurate there
\citep{lassitter2024,hastings2024}; a loading-dependent rate has been fitted by hand
\citep{abuelmaaty2024}. A method that could discover such a law from column data would be
useful. Whether one can, at the noise and isotherm accuracy of a real experiment, is what
this study tests.

We asked, in a pre-registered computational study, for which regimes the adsorption rate law
can be discovered from data a column experiment can produce, and whether discovery fails at the
same boundary as the practical identifiability of the rate constant \citep{raue2009}, or
earlier. Ground truth came from a validated non-isothermal column model \citep{paper1}, for a
Langmuir sorbent and a MOF-303-like step isotherm, over four decades of Damk\"ohler number,
three P\'eclet numbers, four noise levels and four levels of isotherm error, observed through
interior probes of concentration, uptake and temperature, through probes without uptake, or at
the outlet alone. Methods from each family above were run in their strongest available
configuration, and identifiability was judged by profile likelihood
on the same observations. The design, thresholds and verdict words were frozen before any
method ran on the grid.
Discovery failed first. In every slice of the design where both boundaries could be measured,
the law stopped being discovered 1.6 to 3.4 decades of Damk\"ohler number before its rate
constant stopped being identifiable. An isotherm error of 5\,\% prevented discovery at every
rate, and one of 2\,\% in most conditions, while $k$ remained identifiable from interior probes
and, where tested, from the outlet alone.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{Both isotherms pass the positive control}
At the lowest Damk\"ohler number, with no noise and no isotherm error, discovery and
identifiability must both succeed in at least 90\,\% of the estimation replicates, or that
isotherm is withheld from every verdict. Both isotherms passed: the best method recovered the
LDF law in 135 of 135 replicates, and $k$ was identifiable in 135 of 135, for the Langmuir and
for the step isotherm alike. (As first run, the step isotherm failed this check because of a
search-range error in the identifiability code; the correction and both sets of verdicts are
given in Supplementary Note~2.)

\subsection*{Discovery fails long before identifiability}
H2b compares, in each slice of the design (one isotherm, one P\'eclet number, one noise level
and one isotherm error), the Damk\"ohler number at which the best discovery method falls to
50\,\% success, $\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{disc}}$, with the number at which $k$ stops being
identifiable in half the replicates, $\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{ident}}$. Of the 96 slices, 30 had both
boundaries on the grid. In all 30 the verdict was \verdict{discovery fails before
identifiability}, so H2b's pre-registered overall word is the same (Fig.~2, Table~1). The gap
was large everywhere: from 1.6 to 3.4 decades of $\Da$, with a median of 2.3 decades for the
Langmuir sorbent (20 slices) and 2.5 for the step isotherm (10 slices). Discovery failed between
$\Da = 0.11$ and $7.2$, while $k$ stayed identifiable up to $\Da$ between 43 and 562. In every
one of the 30 slices the best discovery method was the same, exact best-subset selection in the
weak form.

held to $\Da \approx 5$ to 7 and identifiability to between the two highest levels ($\Da = 316$

discovery boundary fell to $\Da \approx 1$, and at 5\,\% to $\Da \approx 0.1$ to 0.3, near the
bottom of the grid, while the identifiability boundary stayed between $\Da \approx 57$ and 172.
A small error in the isotherm hurt discovery more than noise did: at 0.5\,\% noise, an isotherm
error of 0.5\,\% moved $\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{disc}}$ from about 6 to about 0.2, more than a decade,
while $\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{ident}}$ moved from 562 to about 360.

The 66 slices without a verdict are not missing data. In each, one of the two probabilities had
no 50\,\% crossing on the grid, and the pre-registered words for that case are
\verdict{no boundary on the grid for one of the two}. A descriptive check made after the verdicts
were seen sorts them by the boundary that is missing. The discovery boundary was missing in
59 of them. In 40, discovery never reached 50\,\% at any $\Da$: all 24 slices with 5\,\%
isotherm error, 15 of the 24 with 2\,\%, and one with 0.5\,\%. In 8, all without noise, it
never fell below 50\,\%. In the other 11, success crossed 50\,\% more than once, so no single
logistic crossing, the pre-registered boundary, describes it (Fig.~2). The clearest case is
symbolic regression (M7) at $\Pe \approx 94$ with 2\,\% isotherm error, the best method in four
slices: its success peaked at 82 to 96\,\% of replicates near $\Da = 3$, in a window between
$\Da = 1$ and 10, and was zero below $\Da = 1$ and above $\Da = 32$. In the remaining 7 slices only the
identifiability boundary was missing, because $k$ stayed identifiable at every level or its
identifiability dipped and recovered at the top of the grid (see below).

descriptive check made after the verdicts were seen. With exact data the

\subsection*{No single error ratio predicts the boundary, and errors-in-variables could not be tested}
H2a asked whether one dimensionless ratio, the realised driving force against the combined
error of the observations and of the isotherm ($R = \delta/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$), places
the discovery boundary at the same value $R^{*}$ in every condition. A verdict needs at least
two conditions whose success crosses 50\,\% in $R$. Only one did (the step isotherm at
$\Pe \approx 19$, observed through all probes, with $R^{*} = 123$, 95\,\% interval 113 to 135),
so H2a has \verdict{no verdict}. In the other eleven conditions success was not a monotone
function of $R$: on probes with uptake (O1) it reached 51 to 71\,\% at single levels of $R$
without a 50\,\% crossing of the fitted curve, and on probes without uptake (O2) it never
exceeded 20\,\%.

% except the one with a crossing); O2 conditions max 0.00-0.20.
% results/p2_verdicts.json H2a (rstar: one condition, O1|step|pe=5.0|ensemble|weak,
% 122.85 [112.60, 135.09]; no_crossing: 11 conditions; words "no verdict: fewer than two
% conditions have a crossing"). pe=5.0 is the D_L x 5 level, Pe about 19.

H2c asked whether an errors-in-variables estimator (M5), which treats the measured isotherm as a
regressor with error, moves the discovery boundary upward. It is tested at 2\,\% noise on probes
with uptake, in the strong form, against the best strong-form method that is not
errors-in-variables, in 18 slices (three isotherm errors from 0.5 to 5\,\%, two isotherms, three
P\'eclet numbers). It holds at an isotherm error if the upward shift is at least 0.25 decade in
two thirds of that error's slices with a verdict, and a slice has a verdict only if both
estimators have a boundary on the grid. No slice had both, so H2c has \verdict{no verdict} at
every isotherm error and is neither supported nor falsified. The reason is the form of the
regression. Errors-in-variables fitting needs the time derivative of the uptake, and in 16 of
the 18 slices the comparator was strong-form best-subset selection, which, like the other
strong-form sparse methods, succeeded in no replicate at any $\Da$ at this noise level; the
errors-in-variables estimator reached at most 40\,\% (step isotherm, 5\,\% isotherm error),
never 50\,\%. In the other two slices ($\Pe \approx 94$, 2\,\% isotherm error) the comparator was
symbolic regression, whose success rose above 50\,\% and fell again within the grid, so it had
no single boundary either. Because the refined grid that H2c would have used is placed around
discovery boundaries, and there were none, it was not solved.

\subsection*{Which methods discover the law, and where}
Exact best-subset selection in the weak form (M4) was the most reliable method: its success
crossed 50\,\% within the grid in 36 of the 96 slices, against 8 for weak-form sequential
thresholding (M2) and 7 for the weak-form ensemble (M3) (Fig.~4c). No strong-form sparse
method had a crossing in any slice. Symbolic
regression (M7), run only at $\Pe \approx 94$ with noise of 0.5 or 2\,\% and isotherm error
of 0 or 2\,\%, behaved differently from all the sparse methods. It failed at the lowest rates,
where weak-form sparse regression worked best, and succeeded at intermediate rates: up to
$\Da \approx 32$ without isotherm error, and between $\Da = 1$ and 10 with 2\,\% isotherm error,
where no sparse method reached 50\,\% (Fig.~4a,b). The
two families therefore cover different ranges of $\Da$, and neither covers the range in which
$k$ remains identifiable. The neural-network pipeline of the closest prior work (M1), run as a
declared demonstration at one noise level, recovered the law in one of its nine fits, at
$\Da = 3.16$. Slow-manifold discovery (M9), which targets the isotherm rather than the rate law,
recovered its own target only at the highest rate and without noise (67\,\% of replicates at
$\Da = 1000$, pooled over isotherms and P\'eclet numbers), and in at most 17\,\% elsewhere.

\subsection*{From the outlet alone, $k$ stays identifiable; the law is not discovered}
Real columns are rarely fitted with probes inside the bed. With concentration and temperature
probes but no uptake (O2), uptake must first be reconstructed from the gas-phase mass balance.
Discovery failed on O2 everywhere: no slice had a 50\,\% boundary, and no method succeeded in
more than 29\,\% of the replicates at any level of any slice.

solve ($\Pe \approx 94$, noise 0.5 and 2\,\%, isotherm error 0 and 2\,\%), held to

\subsection*{A loading-dependent rate is found only where the front crosses the step}
For the step isotherm the study also simulated a rate that falls to 20\,\% of its value across
the isotherm step (L2), a form motivated by the measurements in MOF-303. This part is
exploratory. The
state dependence was discovered, by the pre-registered rule, almost only at $\Da = 1$: there
the best method succeeded in 77 to 100\,\% of replicates at every noise level and isotherm
error, against at most 58\,\% at any other level and none at $\Da \le 0.32$ without noise.
These are the best of all methods and P\'eclet numbers at each level, so they overstate what
a single method achieves. The failure at low $\Da$ is not because the loading never reaches
the step: at $\Da = 0.1$ and 0.32 the loading rose to 41 to 47\,\% of capacity, past the step
midpoint at 36\,\%, and stayed near the step for most of the run. Why the state dependence is
recovered at $\Da = 1$ and not at its neighbours was not resolved here.

\section*{Discussion}
The rate law of a fixed-bed column could be discovered only over a narrow part of the range in
which its rate constant could be measured. In every slice of the design where both boundaries
existed, discovery failed between 1.6 and 3.4 decades of Damk\"ohler number before
identifiability did. With an isotherm error of 5\,\% no method discovered the law at any rate,
and with 2\,\% discovery reached 50\,\% in only 9 of 24 slices, at the lowest rates or, for
symbolic regression, in a narrow window. For
% results/p2_ident_robustness.json / figdata: eps 0.02 slices whose discovery never reaches 50 %: 15 of 24.
practice this sets an order of operations. Where the isotherm is known to better than about
0.5\,\% and the uptake is slow compared with the passage of the front ($\Da$ of order one or
below), sparse regression in the weak form can propose the law. Beyond that, the law has to be
assumed or chosen among a few candidates by model comparison, and the data are then good for
its coefficient over a further one and a half to three decades of $\Da$ from interior probes,
and, at the noise and isotherm errors tested, over roughly one more decade from the outlet alone.

Why is discovery so much more fragile? The two tasks ask different things of the same data.
Identifiability asks whether a known law with one free coefficient fits well only near the true
value. Discovery asks the data to choose that law among many, and, in the success rule used
here, to return the coefficients of $\qsm$ and $q$ in the ratio $-1$ within 10\,\%. An isotherm
error enters discovery as an error in a regressor, which biases exactly that ratio. In the
identifiability fit the same error is absorbed into $k$, and the estimate stays within a factor of
two of the truth. The observation that an isotherm error of 0.5\,\% moved the discovery boundary
by more than a decade, while it moved the identifiability boundary by about 0.2 decade, is
consistent with this reading, although the study was not designed to separate the mechanisms.
A second effect is classical: as $\Da$ grows, uptake approaches equilibrium, the driving force
$\qs - q$ shrinks and the candidate terms become nearly collinear on the slow manifold
\citep{delgado2025,gallo2026}, so the selection step loses contrast before the fit of a single
coefficient does.

The discovery methods were not interchangeable. Exact best-subset selection in the weak form was
the most reliable, and strong-form sparse methods, which differentiate noisy data, never produced
a boundary. Symbolic regression failed where weak-form regression did best, at the lowest rates,
and succeeded at intermediate $\Da$, up to about 32 without isotherm error and between 1 and 10
with 2\,\% isotherm error. A
combination of the two families might widen the range, but it was not tested, and neither family
reached the range where $k$ stayed identifiable. The errors-in-variables estimator could not be
judged, because its strong-form comparator never had a boundary at the noise levels where it was
tested; whether errors-in-variables fitting helps a weak-form search remains open.

The closest prior work recovered the LDF law from noisy outlet curves with a neural network in
the governing equations, with the isotherm given exactly, at one rate \citep{santana2023}. Our
results place that success at one end of a wider map: with an exact isotherm, discovery from
interior probes survives noise of 2 to 5\,\% at low $\Da$, but outside that corner it fails,
and from probes without uptake no method reached 50\,\% at any rate. From the outlet, only
our demonstration of the same neural pipeline (M1) was run; limited by computation to one
noise level, one replicate per rate and fifty optimisation steps, it recovered the law in one
of nine fits. For water in MOF-303, where the rate
depends on loading at the isotherm step \citep{lassitter2024,hastings2024}, a loading-dependent
law was recovered almost only at $\Da = 1$; such a law can be found, but not yet reliably.

The study has limits that bound these conclusions. All data were simulated by one validated
column model with the LDF law as the truth, so the boundaries hold for that model, the two
isotherms and the error models used; real columns add errors in geometry, dispersion and heat
transfer that were held exact here. Observation through interior probes of uptake (O1) is a
ceiling that experiments rarely reach. The identifiability criterion asks whether the 95\,\%
profile interval lies within a factor of two of $k$; it is a criterion of precision and accuracy,
not of calibration. In an exploratory check the intervals contained the true $k$ in only about
10\,\% of rows, because they are narrower than the small biases from time resolution and from
errors in the inputs to $\qsm$. At the two highest $\Da$ levels the time resolution also biased
the estimate itself, by factors of 0.6 to 24, so identifiability there is partly a property of
the sampling; H2b does not depend on those levels. Symbolic regression ran on a subgrid, the
neural pipeline only as a demonstration, and a Kolmogorov--Arnold network only in an exploratory
run on its declared subgrid, where it recovered the law in none of 216 fits; each limit was set
by cost rules declared before the grid ran.
% results/p2_e2_m8.json (216 rows, 0 success, 1 pykan-internal error counted as a failure).
Finally, three errors in our own code and one solver setting that let a solve run for days were
found and corrected during the study; the corrections, and the verdicts as first computed, are
public (Supplementary Note~2), and none changed the main verdict.
% RETRACTIONS_P2 B86 (solve stall, A5), B87, B88, B89 ("Our own error").

Two directions follow. Discovery would gain most from better isotherms: the boundaries moved
more with isotherm error than with noise, so an isotherm measured to a fraction of a per cent is
worth more to discovery than quieter sensors. And the map should be tested where it is most
uncertain, on experimental breakthrough data with interior probes at moderate $\Da$, where it
predicts that discovery should first become possible.

\section*{Methods}

\subsection*{Ground truth}
Ground truth came from the finite-difference reference solver of the companion
study \citep{paper1}: a one-dimensional, axially dispersed, non-isothermal model of a
packed column, verified there against closed-form solutions. Uptake followed one of two
laws,
\begin{align}
\text{L1:}\quad & \frac{\partial q}{\partial t} = k\,\bigl(\qs(c,T) - q\bigr),\\
\text{L2:}\quad & \frac{\partial q}{\partial t} = k(q)\,\bigl(\qs(c,T) - q\bigr),\qquad
k(q) = k_0\Bigl[1 - 0.8\,s\!\Bigl(\frac{q/q_{\max}-\theta}{0.05}\Bigr)\Bigr],
\end{align}
where $s$ is the logistic function and $\theta$ is the fractional loading at the
isotherm's step midpoint, so that under L2 the rate falls to 20\,\% of $k_0$ across the step.
% PREREG_P2 s.4.1; p2_generate.py (L2_DROP 0.8, L2_WIDTH 0.05, step_midpoint_fraction).
Two adsorbents were simulated with the same column geometry and flow (Supplementary
Table~1): the companion study's favourable Langmuir sorbent, and water on a MOF-303-like
framework, a cooperative (Type V) isotherm with its step at $P/P_0 = 0.15$, fed at 30\,\%
relative humidity and 30\,$^\circ$C. The Damk\"ohler number $\Da = k\,t_{\mathrm{stoich}}$
counts kinetic time constants per passage of the adsorption front, with
$t_{\mathrm{stoich}}$ the stoichiometric time of the column (7200\,s for Langmuir and
31\,050\,s for the step isotherm). Nine levels from $10^{-1}$ to $10^{3}$ at half-decade
spacing therefore span $k$ from $1.39\times10^{-5}$ to $0.139$\,s$^{-1}$ for Langmuir and
from $3.22\times10^{-6}$ to $0.0322$\,s$^{-1}$ for the step isotherm. Three axial-dispersion
coefficients, $D_L \times 0.2$, $\times 1$ and $\times 5$ of the default, completed the grid.
They give column P\'eclet numbers $\Pe = uL/D_L$ of about 470, 94 and 19, and the levels are
named by these values below (the code and the pre-registration label them by the $D_L$
multiplier, so its ``$\times 0.2$'' level is the highest $\Pe$). The grid has 81 solves at 400 cells and 400 stored times, each run until the outlet reached 95\,\%
breakthrough, plus 9 isothermal solves for a control described below. The manifest that
records every parameter is public.
% data/p2/manifest.json (t_stoich_s 7200.4 and 31050.4; k_LDF 1.3888e-05..0.13888 and
% 3.2206e-06..0.032206); p2_generate.py N_Z = 400, N_SNAP = 400, 95 % breakthrough rule;
% p2_generate.log "done: 81 cells; without 95 % breakthrough: none".

\subsection*{Observation models, noise and isotherm error}
Every method and every identifiability calculation read the data through one function,
so all saw the same corrupted observations. Three observation models were used: interior
probes of $c$, $q$ and $T$ (O1), interior probes of $c$ and $T$ with $q$ hidden (O2), and the
outlet $c(t)$ and $T(t)$ only (O3). O1 and O2 used 20 equally spaced probes; all models
used 200 times spread evenly over the solved horizon. Noise was independent Gaussian with
standard deviation $\sigma \in \{0, 0.5, 2, 5\}\,\%$ of each channel's range. Methods were
never given the true isotherm. They were given a measured one,
$\qsm(c,T) = \qs(c,T)\,[1 + \varepsilon\, g(c/c_{\mathrm{in}})]$ with
$\varepsilon \in \{0, 0.5, 2, 5\}\,\%$, where $g$ is a smooth random function of four
Fourier modes on $[0, 1]$, scaled to unit root-mean-square and drawn once per replicate.
Every random draw was seeded by a hash: the noise by (cell, replicate, $\sigma$, observation
model) and the isotherm error by (cell, replicate), so each observation can be regenerated on
its own and every observation model sees the same measured isotherm. A replicate, one such
observation of one cell, was the unit of analysis. Discovery on O1 and O2 and identifiability
on O1 used 60 replicates per cell; the costlier O2 and O3 identifiability profiles used 3, and
the M1 demonstration 1.
% p2_observe.py (N_PROBE 20, N_T 200, N_MODES 4, SHA-256 seeding: noise keyed by cell, rep, sigma,
% obs; isotherm error by cell, rep); p2_grid_o23.py (3 replicates); p2_grid_extra.py m1 (1);
% PREREG_P2 s.4.2 and A1 (60 replicates).

\subsection*{Discovery methods}
Each method ran in the strongest configuration we could verify on known-answer systems
before the pre-registration was frozen, and a method that could not be run that way was
reported as such rather than replaced by a weaker one. The candidate library was
$\{1, c, q, T, cq, q^2, c^2, \qsm\}$; under L1 the true law is $k(\qsm - q)$. The methods
were sparse regression on a Savitzky--Golay estimate of $\mathrm{d}q/\mathrm{d}t$ (strong
form) and on its weak, integral form with compactly supported test functions
\citep{messenger2021ode,messenger2021pde}, each with three solvers: sequentially thresholded
least squares \citep{brunton2016}, a bagged ensemble with an inclusion threshold of 0.6
\citep{fasel2022}, and exact best-subset selection by enumeration (M4; exact subset selection
as in \citep{bertsimas2022}, here scored by the Bayesian information criterion); errors-in-variables
best-subset regression given the true error levels (M5, an oracle); implicit sparse
regression without any isotherm on an isothermal control (M6, SINDy-PI \citep{kaheman2020});
symbolic regression (M7, PySR \citep{cranmer2023}); a universal differential equation with a
neural network in the uptake term, trained through the column model and read out by sparse
and symbolic regression (M1, the pipeline of \citep{santana2023}); and regression of the state
on $q$-free features, which returns the isotherm where uptake is at equilibrium (M9
\citep{delgado2025}). On O2, uptake was first recovered from the gas-phase mass balance,
and the three sparse solvers were then run on it in both forms; M5, M7 and M9 were not run on
O2, as the pre-registered applicability table states.
% p2_grid_o23.py (SOLVERS x FORMS); PREREG_P2 s.3 applicability table. M1 could be trained for at most 50 L-BFGS steps or
2\,h per fit on the machine available, so it ran as a declared demonstration: Langmuir,
$\Pe \approx 94$, $\sigma = 2\,\%$, $\varepsilon = 0$, O3, the nine $\Da$ levels, one
replicate. The best parameters seen during training were kept. As first run, a column solve
that exhausted its step budget during training ended the fit with no readout (four of the
nine fits); the corrected code keeps the best parameters in that case, and both runs are
reported (Supplementary Note~2). A Kolmogorov--Arnold network \citep{liu2024} failed its known-answer test and its
cost gate before the freeze and was not run on the grid. Method settings, solver thresholds
and their known-answer tests are listed in Supplementary Note~1.

\subsection*{Success}
Discovery succeeded when the selected terms were exactly those of the true law, every sign
was correct and, for L1, the ratio of the $\qsm$ and $q$ coefficients was $-1$ within
10\,\%. The value of $k$ itself was not required. A method that raised an error counted as
a failure; no replicate, cell or output was excluded.
% p2/metric.py success_L1; PREREG_P2 s.4.3 and A2 (errors as failures).

\subsection*{Identifiability}
Practical identifiability of $k$ was judged by profile likelihood \citep{raue2009} with the
true law known, on the same observations and with the same measured isotherm as the
discovery methods: $k$ was practically identifiable when the 95\,\% profile interval
(likelihood-ratio threshold 3.84) lay within $[k/2, 2k]$. On O1 the likelihood summed over
all probes the fit of the known law, integrated exactly for piecewise-linear $\qs(t)$ along
the observed $c$ and $T$. On O2 and O3 every point of the profile was a full column solve.
The profile searched a coarse logarithmic grid of $k$ and refined it around the interval
(41 points per pass on O1, 21 on O2 and O3, as pre-registered).
Two corrections to the identifiability profiles were made during the study and are reported
in full (Supplementary Note~2). First, on O2 and O3 a measured isotherm with $\varepsilon > 0$ could stall the stiff
solver at large trial $k$ for days; each solve was therefore capped at 600\,s and a capped
solve was scored as a failed solve (amendment A5; two O3 rows had capped solves, none next to
its interval). Second, the O1 grid first spanned $10^{-5}$ to $10$\,s$^{-1}$, which excluded the
step cells at $\Da = 0.1$; it was widened to $10^{-7}$ to $10$\,s$^{-1}$, a peak on the grid
edge was made an error, and all O1 rows were recomputed (amendment A6). Before that
recomputation, the O1 refinement was found to use 21 points per pass instead of the
pre-registered 41 and was set to 41, so every corrected O1 row uses the declared settings.

% SOLVE cap); p2_grid_o23.py SOLVE_CAP_S 600; results/p2_o3_ident.json (n_capped);

\subsection*{Boundaries and verdicts}
For each slice the boundary $\Da^{*}$ was the 50\,\% crossing of an unpenalised logistic
regression of replicate outcomes on $\log_{10}\Da$, with a percentile bootstrap interval
(2000 resamples within each $\Da$ level). No boundary was reported, and no verdict issued,
when the fitted curve did not cross 50\,\% inside the tested range or rose with $\Da$ instead
of falling; the range was reported instead. Before the freeze the estimator's coverage on planted boundaries was 0.94 for a
nominal 0.95, with a bias of $+0.012$ decade. The best discovery method of each slice was
chosen on replicates 0--14 and its boundary estimated on replicates 15--59, which removes the
inflation that choosing and estimating on the same replicates causes (0.07 decade in
simulation); the identifiability boundary was estimated on the same 45 replicates. The best
method was the one with the largest crossing on the selection replicates, or, if none crossed,
the one with the highest mean success. Only rate-law methods that ran on every cell of the
slice competed: M5, which knows the true error levels, and M9, which targets the isotherm, were
excluded.
% p2/analysis.py boundary (n_boot 2000), _fit (falling curve required), select_best (largest
% crossing; fallback highest mean success); p2_analyze.py N_SEL = 15, ORACLE, RATE_LAW_METHODS_EXCLUDED,
% coverage rule (B83, B84);
% tests/test_p2.py::test_boundary_ci_has_nominal_coverage_and_no_bias (0.94, +0.012);
% results/p2_operating_characteristics.json selection bias_same 0.0708; PREREG_P2 A1, A2.

Three hypotheses were confirmatory, each with verdict words fixed in advance. H2a: success
collapses onto one curve in $R = \delta/\varepsilon_{\mathrm{eff}}$, the driving force
relative to the combined error, with the spread of $R^{*}$ across conditions under half a
decade. H2b: on O1, for each slice ($\sigma$, $\varepsilon$, isotherm, $\Pe$; 96 in all),
the gap $\log_{10}\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{disc}} - \log_{10}\Da^{*}_{\mathrm{ident}}$ was called
\verdict{discovery fails before identifiability} at $-0.25$ decade or less,
\verdict{discovery outlives identifiability} at $+0.25$ or more, and
\verdict{discovery fails with identifiability} in between; the hypothesis-level verdict was
the word held by at least two thirds of the slices with a verdict. H2c: errors-in-variables
regression moves the discovery boundary upwards by at least 0.25 decade in at least two thirds
of the slices at a given $\varepsilon > 0$. With 45 estimation replicates the H2b rule
returned a wrong directional verdict with probability 0.001 when there was no gap, and the
correct one with probability 0.965 at a gap of 0.40 decade, the smallest effect of interest.
A positive control guarded every verdict: in each isotherm's easiest cells (lowest $\Da$,
$\sigma = \varepsilon = 0$, O1, the three P\'eclet numbers pooled) the best method had to
succeed and $k$ had to be identifiable in at least 90\,\% of the estimation replicates, or that
isotherm's verdicts were withheld. O2 and O3 were
reported descriptively, and H2d, whether the loading-dependent rate of L2 can be discovered,
was exploratory.

\subsection*{Boundaries with perfect separation}
Where every replicate is identifiable at one level of $\Da$ and none at the next, the
unpenalised logistic fit has no finite maximum-likelihood slope: the optimiser stops at a
steep curve whose crossing lies between the two levels, near their geometric midpoint. Every
bootstrap resample reproduces the same data, so the interval has zero width. Such a boundary
is reported as lying between those two levels; the gap used by H2b is computed from it exactly
as from any other crossing.
